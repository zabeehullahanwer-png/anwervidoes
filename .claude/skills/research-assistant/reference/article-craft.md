# Article craft

## 1. Is there a contribution?

A reviewer asks one question: *what do I know after this paper that I did not know before?*
Write the answer in one sentence before writing anything else.

Kinds of contribution that work in Islamic studies:

| Kind | Example shape |
|---|---|
| New text / neglected source | first analytical study of a manuscript, an Urdu work, a scholar's unstudied treatise |
| New reading | re-reading a known text with a method or question earlier work did not use |
| Comparison | two scholars, schools, madhahib, tafasir, or classical vs. modern on one narrow point |
| Application | a usul / maqasid / qawa'id framework applied to a concrete contemporary issue (finance, bioethics, digital media, family law in Pakistan) |
| Critique | testing an orientalist or modernist claim against the primary sources |
| Empirical | survey / interviews / content analysis (madaris, mosques, social media da'wah) with a clear method |
| Synthesis | a mapped literature review of a field, with a gap agenda (good for international journals) |

Red flags: a topic as wide as a book ("the role of Islam in society"); a descriptive summary of a scholar's
life; no secondary literature since 2015; no question, only a theme.

Feasibility test: sources in hand? doable in 3–6 weeks? fits 6,000–8,000 words? fits the target journal's scope?

## 2. Structures

**Humanities / Islamic-studies article (most HEC journals):**

1. Title (specific; often "Main claim: scope, method or source")
2. Abstract (150–250 words) + 4–6 keywords; Urdu-language journals usually need an **English abstract** too
3. Introduction: context → problem → research question(s) → significance → method → structure of the paper
4. Literature review (sometimes "previous studies" / سابقہ کام کا جائزہ) ending in the gap
5. Body sections, each with one claim, evidence, analysis
6. Discussion / analysis (what the findings mean, against the literature)
7. Conclusion: findings in numbered form, contribution, limits, recommendations, future research
8. References / footnotes in the journal's style

**IMRaD (empirical studies):** Introduction, Literature, Methodology (design, sample, instrument, analysis,
ethics), Results, Discussion, Conclusion.

## 3. Abstract formula (5–6 sentences)

1. Context and problem. 2. The gap. 3. Aim / question. 4. Method and sources. 5. Main findings (concrete).
6. Contribution / implication. Never cite in the abstract; no "this paper will try to".

Urdu abstract (خلاصہ) follows the same moves. Keep the English and Urdu abstracts consistent in content.

## 4. Writing quality (what makes it read as a scholar, not a machine)

- Make an argument. Each paragraph: a claim (topic sentence), evidence, analysis, link to the next.
- Be specific: names, dates, editions, page numbers, the exact Arabic term. Vague generality is the
  biggest sign of weak and of machine-made writing.
- Vary sentence length naturally; let a short sentence carry an important point.
- Show disagreement with sources where warranted ("Ibn ʿĀshūr's reading, however, rests on ...").
- Use the author's own judgement and voice: "we argue", "this study finds" (as the journal prefers).
- Avoid filler and stock phrases (English: "delve into", "in today's world", "it is important to note",
  "plays a pivotal role", "a rich tapestry", "in conclusion, it can be said"; Urdu: "یہ بات قابلِ ذکر ہے کہ"
  repeated, "موجودہ دور میں اس کی اہمیت اور بھی بڑھ جاتی ہے", "خلاصۂ کلام یہ کہ" in every section). The
  `style` command lists them.
- Quote primary sources in the original (Arabic) with translation; keep quotations short and analysed.
- Terms: define once, use consistently. Transliteration: one system throughout.

## 5. Titles

Good: *"Maṣlaḥa in Pakistani Family Courts: A Usūlī Reading of Khula Judgments (2015–2024)"*.
Weak: *"Importance of Maslaha in Islam"*.

## 6. Keywords

4–6; mix the field term, the specific concept, the source/person, the context and the method.
Avoid repeating title words only.
